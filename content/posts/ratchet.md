There's a man I'd like you to picture.

Not a stereotype. A specific guy. He learned to text back without a four-day gap. He learned to say "I love you" first and not hold it hostage. He learned to cook on a Tuesday, to notice his partner had a bad day, to ask "are you okay?" and actually mean it. He did the work. He changed.

And here's the thing that keeps me up at night: **it stopped counting the moment it became normal.**

## The trap is real

For a hundred years, the male default was a script. Provide, withdraw, compartmentalize, be "the strong one," show up physically but not emotionally. A guy who followed that script wasn't being a monster — he was being *trained*. The script was so universal it was invisible, like breathing.

Women noticed. They noticed for a very long time. And what women noticed is exactly what this essay is about: when a man *broke* the script, when he started showing up in the way the script never demanded — that moment was a gift. A delta. A real improvement over the baseline he was handed.

But then he did it again. And again. And it became his normal. And the delta vanished into the baseline. And the gift was gone. Not because she stopped loving him — because the *novelty premium* that motivated the change evaporated the instant the habit formed.

That's the ratchet. It clicks forward one tooth when he improves. Then it locks in place, and the tooth becomes the new floor. And the floor is never celebrated. Floors are just where you stand.

## So why should she celebrate?

Here's the question you asked, and I think it's the right one, and I think it has an uncomfortable answer.

Your instinct is: *he climbed the mountain, and I should applaud the summit, not the base camp.* And you're right. If the baseline is the problem, then moving above the baseline is the victory, and burying that victory under "well, you should've always done that" is exactly the flattening that killed the motivation.

But here's where I push back on myself, because I don't want to write a vent and call it an insight. The ratchet is real, but it's also *self-serving* unless we test it against its own logic. And when I test it, three problems show up.

### 1. "Distance" might not be ingratitude. It might be feedback.

The essay assumes her coolness is a *failure to reward*. But what if it's not? What if he changed, but he changed *badly*? What if his "showing up" is performative — the texts are fast but shallow, the affection is on display but not consistent, the improvement is real but it's the *minimum* version of it?

If that's the case, her distance isn't ungrateful. It's *accurate*. She's not ignoring his climb; she's reading the altitude correctly and finding it lower than he thinks it is. The problem isn't that she doesn't celebrate the change — it's that the change is smaller than the story.

This matters because it separates two diagnoses that look identical from the outside: "men aren't changing" (his fault) versus "men changed but went unrewarded" (the framing's fault). Your essay is about the *second* one, and it's the stronger claim — but it only holds if we grant that his change was genuinely substantial. The moment a reader suspects it was performative, the ratchet argument collapses into the simpler accusation that he's coasting on a half-effort.

### 2. A moving goalpost is only a goalpost if there's an objective bar.

The ratchet logic only works if there's a standard he's *supposed* to meet. But here's the catch: when he levels the field, when he brings himself up to the baseline, that baseline is *allowed to move*. The floor is supposed to rise. The fact that the floor rose *because of him* is precisely the point of leveling the field.

So the complaint "now the bar moved and I have to do more" is only a complaint if the bar was *supposed to stay still*. But it wasn't. The whole mechanism of the ratchet is that it advances. The question is whether it advanced *fairly* — whether the new bar was reasonable, or whether it advanced too far too fast, leaving him chasing a target that keeps retreating.

And here's the deeper issue: **whose bar is it?** If the bar is "what I personally want from you," then it's subjective, and it moves every time I want something new. If the bar is "what's reasonable for a partner," then it's more objective, but it still moves as culture evolves, and it moves *because people like him raised it*. So he's being blamed for the thing that made the bar possible in the first place.

This is where the essay gets most complicated, and where I think it's most interesting. Because it's not really about "women don't appreciate men." It's about **whether the appreciation was ever going to be sustainable** once the change became the norm. And I don't think it was. Not because the appreciation was fake, but because the *motivation* for the change was inherently temporary. The moment the change was his identity, it stopped being a delta, and the delta is what made it valuable.

So the real question isn't "why don't women celebrate when men change?" It's "**can women even celebrate a change that becomes the new normal?**" And I don't think they can. Not because they're ungrateful, but because a change that's become normal is, by definition, no longer a change. It's just the situation. And you don't celebrate situations. You just live in them.

That's the ratchet, and it's the thing that keeps the men in my head stuck. They did the work. The work paid off. The work is now the baseline. The baseline isn't celebrated. The baseline is just there. And the man who built the baseline is standing in it, wondering why the applause stopped.

The answer is: **the applause wasn't for the baseline. It was for the climb.** And the climb is over. The climb is the whole point. The moment you stop climbing, you're just standing. And standing is fine, but it's not the summit. And nobody claps for standing.

So what do you do? Do you stop climbing and just stand? Or do you keep climbing, knowing there's no summit, knowing the next summit is the one that's currently the baseline, knowing you have to keep going because stopping is the only real loss?
